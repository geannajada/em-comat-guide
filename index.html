The One Algorithm That Solves Half the Test VERY HIGH YIELD
Is the patient stable?
Unstable
  • Shock, peritonitis, tension physiology, active hemorrhage, hard signs of vascular injury → treat/decompress/OR now, skip imaging
  • Bedside US (FAST/E-FAST, cardiac) is the only "imaging" allowed before stabilizing
Stable
  • Time for a deliberate workup: targeted labs, EKG, then imaging (US → CT)
  • Best initial test = cheapest/fastest test that changes management
Board trap"Unstable, what's next?" is never another scan — it's an intervention (decompress, transfuse, intubate, OR). See the Unstable Patient tab for the full syndrome-by-syndrome breakdown.

Question Decoder what is the stem actually asking?

Translate common COMAT phrasing into the correct reasoning task before you touch the answer choices. This is the fastest fix for rereading stems and rushing.

Stem Phrase → Reasoning Task VERY HIGH YIELD
"Most likely diagnosis"Identify the single diagnosis that best explains the entire presentation — not just one finding.
"Which of the following best explains..."Identify the diagnosis, mechanism, or pathophysiology that best accounts for the findings.
"Best initial test"What is the first practical diagnostic test given this patient's CURRENT clinical state?
"Best screening test"First test to rule out or detect the condition in the appropriate setting.
"Most accurate test"The definitive or most sensitive/specific test — even if it isn't performed first.
"Confirmatory test"What test establishes the diagnosis after initial suspicion or screening.
"Best imaging modality"Modality driven by diagnosis, stability, trauma status, and urgency.
"Most appropriate initial step"What must happen FIRST — often stabilization before further diagnostics.
"Best next step"What has ALREADY happened in the stem, and what logically follows?
"Patient remains hypotensive/symptomatic despite..."Initial therapy failed — escalate treatment rather than repeating the same step reflexively.
"Definitive treatment"What ultimately fixes the underlying problem after stabilization.
"Which finding indicates severity?"Identify the red flag or threshold that changes management or disposition.
"Which finding warrants admission/ICU/OR?"Identify the feature that changes disposition.
"Which of the following is contraindicated?"Identify the dangerous or prohibited option — not merely the less-preferred choice.
"Which complication is most likely?"Think about the classic downstream consequence of the disease or treatment.
"Which patient can be discharged?"Is the patient stable, low-risk, adequately evaluated, and safe for outpatient care?
"Which requires immediate intervention?"Look for instability, airway compromise, shock, hemorrhage, severe neuro deterioration, or another time-critical threat.
"Which test should be obtained next?"Use the patient's CURRENT stage of evaluation, not the diagnosis in isolation.
"Which treatment should be given next?"Use the management sequence already described in the stem.
"Which medication is most appropriate?"Consider indication + contraindications + current stability + board-standard first-line therapy.
"Which medication should be avoided?"Look for a high-yield contraindication, toxic interaction, or treatment that worsens the disease.
"Which of the following should be avoided or reversed?"Look for: a contraindication, a reversal agent, an antidote, an unsafe medication, or a therapy that must be stopped — this phrasing signals anticoagulation reversal or antidote selection specifically.
BEFORE ANSWERING, ask in order:
  1. What is the patient's stability?
  2. What has already been done?
  3. Is this asking for diagnosis, first test, best test, first treatment, next treatment, definitive treatment, or disposition?
  4. Is there a dangerous contraindication or board trap?
  5. What answer is correct at THIS exact point in the algorithm?

Cardiovascular / Circulatory 11-14%

Chest pain, arrhythmias, syncope, and vascular emergencies. Every stable chest pain patient gets an EKG within 10 minutes of arrival.

Acute Coronary Syndrome (STEMI/NSTEMI/UA) VERY HIGH YIELD
STEMI
Mechanism / whyAtherosclerotic plaque rupture → platelet activation + acute coronary thrombosis → transmural ischemia when flow is fully occluded.
Second-orderWatch for papillary-muscle rupture, VSD, free-wall rupture, ventricular arrhythmia, or cardiogenic shock after MI; the timing + new murmur/hemodynamic change is often the tested clue.
RecognizeSubsternal pressure ± radiation, diaphoresis; ST elevation ≥1mm in ≥2 contiguous leads
Best initial testEKG within 10 minutes of arrival → troponin
Best initial stepAspirin (chew) + O2 if hypoxic + P2Y12 inhibitor + heparin
Definitive treatmentPCI <90 min (door-to-balloon) or fibrinolysis <30 min if PCI unavailable/>120min transfer
ContraindicationFibrinolysis absolute: prior ICH, ischemic stroke <3mo, active bleeding, suspected aortic dissection, severe uncontrolled HTN not responding to tx. Relative: BP>180/110, recent major surgery/GI bleed <3wk, current anticoagulation — prefer PCI when any of these are present
Do NOTNitrates/diuretics/opioids if RV infarct (inferior MI, right-sided ST elevation) — preload-dependent, will crash BP; give IV fluids instead

Aortic Dissection VERY HIGH YIELD
Aortic dissection
Mechanism / whyIntimal tear lets blood enter the media → false lumen; branch-vessel obstruction explains pulse/BP asymmetry and end-organ ischemia.
Second-orderProximal extension can cause acute aortic regurgitation, coronary ischemia, or tamponade. Type A disease generally requires emergent surgery.
Stem clues / setupUncontrolled HTN (most common risk factor), Marfan/Ehlers-Danlos, bicuspid aortic valve, cocaine use, pregnancy (3rd trimester), prior cardiac surgery
RecognizeTearing/ripping chest/back pain, unequal BP or pulses between limbs, widened mediastinum on CXR
Best initial testCXR (widened mediastinum) + EKG (rule out MI mimic)
Best/confirmatory testCTA chest/abdomen (stable); TEE if renal disease or too unstable to leave the ED
Best initial stepHR/BP control FIRST — esmolol (rate) before nitroprusside (pressure); never vasodilate before rate control (reflex tachycardia worsens shear)
Definitive treatmentStanford A (ascending) → emergent surgery. Stanford B (descending) → medical management unless malperfusion/rupture
Board trapDo not anticoagulate — this is a surgical/BP-control disease, not a clotting disease (unlike ACS/PE)

WPW (Wolff-Parkinson-White)
Mechanism / whyAccessory atrioventricular pathway (bundle of Kent) bypasses the AV node → ventricular pre-excitation. The delta wave is the slurred early upstroke from premature ventricular activation.
RecognizeShort PR + delta wave + widened QRS at baseline; AFib with WPW = irregular, very rapid, wide-complex rhythm
Do NOTDo NOT give AV nodal blockers (adenosine, verapamil/diltiazem, beta blockers, digoxin) in pre-excited AF — blocking the AV node favors conduction down the accessory pathway and can precipitate VF
ManagementStable pre-excited AF: procainamide or ibutilide. Unstable: synchronized cardioversion. Recurrent symptomatic WPW → catheter ablation.

HEART score — chest pain risk stratification & disposition

Scores History, EKG, Age, Risk factors, Troponin at 0/1/2 points each. 0-3 = low risk (safe for discharge with outpatient follow-up); 4-6 = moderate (admit/observe, further testing); ≥7 = high risk (admit, early invasive strategy).

Mechanism / whyCystic-duct obstruction (usually a stone) → gallbladder distention/inflammation; this is why pain localizes to RUQ and Murphy sign is useful.

Appendicitis & Mesenteric Ischemia VERY HIGH YIELD
Acute appendicitis
Mechanism / whyLuminal obstruction → distention and visceral periumbilical pain; later parietal-peritoneal irritation causes migration to the RLQ.
Second-orderDelay increases perforation/abscess risk; imaging choice changes with age/pregnancy rather than the underlying diagnosis.
Stem cuePain periumbilical → migrates to RLQ (McBurney's point), anorexia, low-grade fever, N/V after pain onset
Context matters — imagingAdult, nonpregnant → CT abd/pelvis with contrast is the preferred initial/most accurate test (highest sensitivity/specificity). Pregnant or pediatric → graded compression ultrasound first to avoid radiation
If ultrasound is nondiagnosticPediatric: proceed to CT if suspicion remains high. Pregnant: MRI without contrast preferred over CT for further workup to avoid fetal radiation exposure

Acute mesenteric ischemia
Mechanism / whyUsually arterial embolus (often AF), thrombosis, or low-flow ischemia → bowel hypoperfusion before peritoneal inflammation develops.
Second-orderPain out of proportion to exam is early; lactate may be normal initially. CTA is the key diagnostic study in a stable patient.

Second-orderSubcutaneous emphysema, pleural effusion, pneumomediastinum, and rapid sepsis are classic downstream clues; this differs from a mucosal Mallory-Weiss tear.

Sepsis / Septic Shock HIGH YIELD
  • Sepsis: life-threatening organ dysfunction from dysregulated host response to infection (SOFA ↑≥2)
  • Septic shock: sepsis + hypotension needing vasopressors to MAP ≥65 + lactate >2 despite adequate fluids
Sepsis bundle (order matters)
  1. Blood cultures x2 (before abx, don't delay abx to get them)
  2. Antibiotics immediately, ideally ≤1 h for septic shock or probable/high-likelihood sepsis; if possible sepsis without shock, rapidly evaluate mimics/source and give antibiotics within ~3 h if infection remains likely.
  3. 30 mL/kg crystalloid bolus for hypotension/lactate ≥4
  4. Still hypotensive after fluids → norepinephrine to MAP ≥65
  5. Source control
Septic shock
Mechanism / whyDysregulated host response → vasodilation + capillary leak + maldistributed flow; myocardial depression may coexist.
Second-orderPersistent hypotension after adequate fluid resuscitation → norepinephrine; complications include AKI, ARDS, DIC, and myocardial dysfunction.

Best initial stepBroad-spectrum IV abx within 1 hour of recognition — don't wait on cultures to return
Next step30mL/kg crystalloid; still hypotensive after fluids → norepinephrine to MAP≥65

Massive Transfusion & Trauma-Induced Coagulopathy

MTP: ≥2 of penetrating trauma, positive FAST, HR≥120, SBP≤90 → 1:1:1 ratio, ≥6 units each. O-negative until type/cross available.

Lethal triad
Hypothermia (warm fluids/blood) + Coagulopathy (dilution — minimize crystalloid) + Acidosis (hypoperfusion/excess NS) — each worsens the others.

Other complications: hypocalcemia (citrate chelation — give calcium gluconate), TRALI (non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema <6h, doesn't respond to diuretics) vs TACO (volume overload, responds to diuretics).

One-liner: shock questions test whether you can match a hemodynamic/exam pattern to a category, then sequence the right resuscitation (fluids vs pressors vs source control).

Hemoptysis CORE

Distinguish first: true hemoptysis (from the airway, often frothy/bright red, mixed with sputum) vs. hematemesis (from the GI tract, dark/coffee-ground, with N/V) vs. nasopharyngeal bleeding (posterior epistaxis draining into the pharynx).

Common causes: infection (bronchitis most common overall), TB, malignancy, PE, bronchiectasis.

Board trapMassive/life-threatening hemoptysis is defined by physiologic threat and airway risk, not an arbitrary volume cutoff — prioritize airway protection, oxygenation, and hemodynamic stabilization first. If the bleeding side is known, position the bleeding lung dependent (down) to protect the healthy lung from aspirating blood. Definitive control: bronchoscopy or bronchial artery embolization.

Definition: fluid accumulation in alveolar spaces from elevated pulmonary capillary pressure (cardiogenic) or increased capillary permeability (non-cardiogenic/ARDS).

Drowning & High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema HIGH YIELD
Drowning injury
Mechanism / whyHypoxemia is the primary injury; aspiration disrupts surfactant and gas exchange → noncardiogenic pulmonary edema/ARDS. Fresh vs salt water distinctions are not clinically useful for resuscitation.
Best initial priorityVentilation and oxygenation. In cardiac arrest after drowning, provide rescue breaths + chest compressions; use oxygen when available.
ComplicationsDelayed pulmonary edema/ARDS, aspiration pneumonitis, hypothermia, dysrhythmia, cerebral hypoxic injury; consider trauma if mechanism suggests diving/impact.
Board trapsDo not perform routine abdominal thrusts to “remove water”; they delay ventilation and increase aspiration risk. Prophylactic antibiotics are not routine unless there is clear infection/highly contaminated exposure.

Mechanism / whyHypobaric hypoxia → exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction → high pulmonary artery pressure and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema.

Board Traps — Consolidated the classic ways this exam tries to trick you

Format: TRAP → correct board reasoning. These also appear locally under their relevant system tab — this page is your final-pass sweep.

First Test vs Best Test / Screening vs Diagnostic VERY HIGH YIELD
  • Testicular torsion → clinical diagnosis; don't delay surgery for Doppler US if suspicion is high.
  • Compartment syndrome → clinical diagnosis (pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch); don't wait for needle manometry or pulselessness.
  • Necrotizing fasciitis → clinical diagnosis + surgical exploration; imaging only delays definitive debridement.
  • Tension pneumothorax → clinical diagnosis; do not wait for CXR before decompressing.
Stabilization vs Diagnostics vs Definitive Treatment VERY HIGH YIELD
  • "Unstable, what's next?" is never another scan — it's an intervention (decompress, transfuse, intubate, OR).
  • DKA: insulin is never started before you know the potassium — hypokalemic patients risk fatal arrhythmia as K+ shifts intracellularly.
  • Hyperkalemia with ECG changes: calcium gluconate to stabilize the membrane comes before insulin/glucose to shift potassium.
  • Blunt aortic injury survivors: gentle rate/BP control (esmolol first) before repair — aggressive resuscitation can convert a contained rupture into a free one.
Unstable vs Stable Recognition HIGH YIELD
  • Hypotension is a LATE finding in hemorrhage (typically Class III+, >30% blood loss) — tachycardia/anxiety come first.
  • Neurogenic shock = hypotension + bradycardia + warm skin. Hemorrhagic shock = hypotension + tachycardia + cool skin — don't mix these up.
  • Warm, well-perfused extremities + hypotension = distributive shock (sepsis/anaphylaxis/neurogenic/adrenal crisis) almost every time.
  • Compartment syndrome: pulselessness is a LATE finding — pain out of proportion and pain with passive stretch come first.
Contraindications & Dangerous Medications VERY HIGH YIELD
  • Right-sided MI: avoid nitrates, diuretics, opioids — they crash preload-dependent RV output. Treat with IV fluids.
  • 3rd degree heart block: atropine is usually ineffective (escape rhythm is ventricular, distal to the AV node) — go to pacing.
  • Pheochromocytoma / cocaine toxicity: never give a beta blocker first — unopposed alpha causes a hypertensive emergency. Alpha-block first.
  • Acute ischemic stroke: do NOT aggressively lower BP (permissive hypertension preserves the penumbra) — the opposite rule applies in hemorrhagic stroke.
  • Myasthenic crisis: avoid aminoglycosides, beta blockers, and magnesium — they worsen neuromuscular transmission.
Imaging & Test-Selection Traps HIGH YIELD
  • FAST does NOT reliably detect retroperitoneal bleeding (pelvic fracture, renal injury) — a negative FAST doesn't rule out major hemorrhage there.
  • Pulse ox is falsely normal in both CO poisoning and methemoglobinemia — need co-oximetry to catch these.
  • Chemical eye burn: irrigate FIRST, before checking visual acuity or asking detailed history.
  • New breast mass >30-35y, or bloody nipple discharge at any age — needs imaging, don't dismiss as fibroadenoma.
Anchoring & Physiology Traps HIGH YIELD
  • Rigid, "quiet," board-like abdomen = peritonitis until proven otherwise — surgery now, not more labs.
  • Strangulated vs incarcerated hernia: strangulation (vascular compromise) is a surgical emergency — don't attempt manual reduction if ischemia/peritonitis signs present.
  • Hypertensive (flash) pulmonary edema is an afterload problem, not volume overload — nitrates/BiPAP first-line, not aggressive diuresis alone.
  • Epiglottitis / pediatric airway foreign body: never agitate the child or force a throat exam — can precipitate complete obstruction.
  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: diagnosis of exclusion in chronic heavy cannabis users; cessation of use is the only definitive treatment — don't keep escalating antiemetics.
Pharmacology & Sequencing Traps VERY HIGH YIELD
  • WPW + AFib → irregular, very rapid, wide-complex rhythm — AV nodal blockers (adenosine, verapamil/diltiazem, beta blockers) can precipitate VF; use procainamide/ibutilide (stable) or cardioversion (unstable) instead.
  • "No contraindications" is not a real answer choice for thrombolysis — know the actual absolute contraindications (prior ICH, recent ischemic stroke/major surgery/bleed, suspected dissection, uncontrolled severe HTN) before picking tPA.
  • Major bleeding on anticoagulation → reverse the SPECIFIC agent (vitamin K + PCC for warfarin, idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet for Xa inhibitors, protamine for heparin) — don't reflexively give FFP for everything.
  • Thyroid storm: iodine BEFORE a thionamide can transiently worsen thyrotoxicosis (Jod-Basedow effect) — beta blocker → thionamide → iodine, in that order, every time.
  • TTP: avoid reflexive platelet transfusion — can worsen the microthrombosis; start plasma exchange instead.
  • Meningitis with signs of mass effect/herniation risk → give empiric antibiotics first, then CT, then LP if safe — never delay antibiotics to obtain imaging, and never LP first if herniation risk is present.
  • Hyperkalemia: calcium does NOT lower serum potassium — it only stabilizes the myocardial membrane. Don't skip it in a patient with ECG changes just because it "doesn't fix the number."
  • Chronic hyponatremia → correcting too rapidly risks osmotic demyelination syndrome — even in a symptomatic patient needing hypertonic saline, the total 24-hour rise still needs to stay within a safe limit.
  • Chronic hypernatremia → correcting too rapidly risks cerebral edema — free water replacement must be gradual, not immediate full correction.
  • Malignant hyperthermia: don't wait for extreme hyperthermia to act — rising end-tidal CO2 and muscle rigidity intraoperatively are earlier, more reliable signs; give dantrolene and stop the trigger immediately.
  • Boerhaave vs Mallory-Weiss: Mallory-Weiss is a mucosal tear with hematemesis and no perforation; Boerhaave is a transmural rupture and surgical/mediastinal emergency — don't manage a suspected Boerhaave as a routine upper GI bleed.
  • Appendicitis imaging: ultrasound-first is not a universal rule — CT is preferred initial imaging in nonpregnant adults; ultrasound is first-line only in pregnancy/pediatrics specifically to avoid radiation.
  • Impaled objects: never remove in the field or ED — stabilize in place; removal can release tamponade on a lacerated vessel and cause fatal hemorrhage.
  • Globe rupture vs orbital compartment syndrome: IOP measurement is contraindicated in suspected globe rupture but indicated and important in orbital compartment syndrome — know which one you're facing.
  • Giant cell arteritis: start steroids immediately if vision is threatened — do not wait for temporal artery biopsy; delay risks irreversible vision loss.
  • Hypercapnic COPD failure: don't withhold oxygen for fear of "removing hypoxic drive" — give controlled, titrated O2; the real risk is worsened V/Q mismatch and the Haldane effect, not apnea.
  • Infected/obstructing kidney stone → true urologic emergency — emergent decompression, not just antibiotics and pain control.
One-liner: nearly every board trap reduces to one of three errors — treating a diagnosis before stabilizing, treating a stage of workup that's already passed, or anchoring on the common diagnosis despite a discriminating clue.

Qbank-Depth Pearls — compiled
  • Parkland formula: 2 mL x %TBSA x weight(kg) over first 24h for 2nd-degree+ burns (updated ATLS/ABA value; classic teaching says 4 mL/kg) — half over first 8h from time of injury, titrate to UOP ≥0.5 mL/kg/hr.
  • DKA management order: fluids first (isotonic saline ~1L/hr) → insulin (hold if K+<3.3) → potassium repletion if ≤5.3 → bicarb only if pH<6.9.
  • 90% of blunt aortic injuries are fatal at the scene — rupture at the isthmus where the mobile arch meets the fixed descending aorta.
  • PERC rule only applies to patients already deemed low pretest probability for PE — all 8 criteria negative rules out PE without needing a D-dimer.
  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: diagnosis of exclusion in chronic heavy cannabis users; cessation of use is the only definitive treatment.
  • Hyperkalemia framework: stabilize (calcium — protects the heart, doesn't lower K+) → shift (insulin/glucose, albuterol, bicarb) → eliminate (diuretics, kayexalate, dialysis).
  • Severe symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, seizures, prolonged QT) → IV calcium; check magnesium if refractory.
  • CO poisoning: house fire/enclosed generator/faulty heater + multiple household members with vague symptoms → co-oximetry (standard pulse ox is falsely normal) → 100% O2, hyperbaric if severe.
  • HEART score: 0-3 low risk (safe for discharge), 4-6 moderate (admit/observe), ≥7 high risk (admit, early invasive strategy) — components: History, EKG, Age, Risk factors, Troponin.
  • Ottawa ankle/knee rules: ankle — X-ray if malleolar tenderness + can't bear weight/bony tenderness at malleolar tip; knee — X-ray if age ≥55, patellar tenderness, fibular head tenderness, can't flex 90°, or can't bear weight.
  • TTP pentad (when present): thrombocytopenia + microangiopathic hemolytic anemia + fever + renal failure + neuro changes — emergent plasma exchange, avoid platelet transfusion.
  • Hemophilia lab pattern: isolated ↑PTT, normal PT, normal platelets, deep/joint bleeding — replace the specific factor.
  • Wallenberg (lateral medullary) syndrome: dysphagia + hoarseness + ipsilateral Horner + crossed sensory loss (ipsilateral face, contralateral body) — PICA/vertebral artery.
  • ICH by location: basal ganglia (most common, HTN) • pons (coma, pinpoint pupils) • cerebellum (vertigo/ataxia, risk of herniation) • lobar (consider amyloid angiopathy in elderly).
  • Massive hemoptysis: position the bleeding lung dependent (down) to protect the healthy lung — prioritize airway/oxygenation/hemodynamics over an arbitrary volume cutoff.
